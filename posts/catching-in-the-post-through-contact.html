title: How to catch the ball in the post through contact date: 2026-11-01 minutes: 5 art: dropstep summary: Post moves don't matter if you can't hold the catch. Here's how to build strong hands, body position and footwork so players keep the ball through contact. ---

Every post move I teach depends on one thing that happens first: the catch. I've watched a lot of young players win great position, call for the ball, and then lose it the second a defender bumps them. They don't need a new move. They need to learn how to catch through contact. It's a skill, not toughness you're born with, and it can be built one piece at a time.

Why the catch gets lost

It's usually one of three things: standing too tall, so the bump moves the whole body; reaching with the arms out, so the defender can swipe it; or soft hands that let the ball bounce off the palms. Fix those three and the catch gets much more reliable.

The foundation: a stance that can take a hit

Before we ever throw a ball, I want players in a base that doesn't move when someone leans on it.

  • Feet wider than your shoulders. A wide base is harder to push off the block. Narrow feet mean you get moved.
  • Knees bent, hips low. Whoever is lower usually wins the contact. Your hips should feel like they're sitting into the defender.
  • Chest up, eyes on the passer. Bending at the waist pulls you off balance and takes your eyes off the ball.

I push on a player's shoulder and hip from different angles. If they stumble, we widen and lower until they don't. Most players skip this, and everything after it falls apart.

Step one: hands ready before the pass

Once the stance holds, we add the hands. Your hands should be up and open before the ball is thrown, not after.

  • Fingers spread wide and pointed up, thumbs close together, making a target.
  • Show the target on the side away from your defender. That tells your guard where the pass is safe.
  • Keep your elbows bent a little. Straight, locked arms are easy for a defender to knock down.

Why? A post entry pass takes a fraction of a second. If your hands come up while the ball is in the air, you're late, and a late catch is a lost catch.

Step two: meet the ball and catch with two hands

Don't wait for the ball to come to you. Step toward it with your top foot and meet it, while your backside keeps your defender sealed.

Catch with two hands and pull the ball in strong. I tell players to "catch it like it's trying to escape." Fingers grab the ball, not the palms. Then go right into the chin position: ball under your chin, elbows out, fingers on top and back of the ball.

Why it works: Meeting the ball shortens the pass, so there's less time for the defender to get a hand on it. Chinning the ball puts it close to your body and away from reaching hands. A defender can bump you, but it's very hard to strip a ball that's tucked under your chin with your elbows protecting it.

Step three: land balanced and keep your pivot

A lot of post turnovers aren't steals. They're travels: a player gets bumped, shuffles their feet, and the whistle blows.

I want the catch to finish with a balanced two-foot landing or a firm stop on both feet, knees still bent. Now you have a choice of pivot foot, and you're ready to make a move. If the defender is pushing hard, stay low and let them lean. That pressure is information. It tells you which way they're overplaying, and that's the side you'll attack.

Adding pressure, one level at a time

Once the pieces are solid, we make it harder in small steps.

  1. Light contact. A partner leans on you with a pad or a forearm while you catch easy passes. Focus on staying low and chinning every ball.
  2. Harder bumps. The partner bumps you right as the ball arrives. This is the moment that matters in a game, so we rep it a lot.
  3. Faster, tougher passes. Bounce passes, passes a little off target, passes thrown with more zip.
  4. Live reps. Now the defender is really trying to deny and knock the ball away. Catch, chin, read, and finish with a move like the drop step.

Don't move up a level until the current one looks easy. Pressure only helps once the habit is there.

How to practice at home

Fifteen minutes a few times a week is enough.

  • Stance check: Get in your post stance and have a parent or sibling push from different angles. Ten seconds, five times. Don't let them move you.
  • Wall catches: Throw the ball hard off a wall, step to meet it, catch with two hands and chin it. Twenty catches.
  • Pillow bumps: Have a partner hold a couch cushion and bump you as they toss you the ball. Catch, chin, land on two feet. Fifteen reps.
  • Finish it: If you have a hoop, add one move after each catch so the catch always leads to something.

As a post player at BYU and Utah Valley, I learned fast that the strongest defenders weren't trying to block my shot. They were trying to make sure I never got a clean catch. Players who hold the ball through contact get more touches, draw more fouls, and play with more confidence. It all starts with a low stance and strong hands.
