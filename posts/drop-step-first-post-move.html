When parents ask what their player should learn first in the post, my answer is almost always the same: the drop step. It isn't flashy. It's one step and a finish. But it works in third grade and it works in college, and once a player understands it, every other post move gets easier.

What a drop step is

A drop step is a post move where you "drop" the foot closest to the baseline back and around your defender, then step toward the rim to score. You're not trying to get past the defender with speed. You're using the position you already won to get to the basket in one step.

It starts before the catch

Most young players think the move starts when they get the ball. It doesn't. A drop step only works if you've won position first.

  • Get low and wide. Feet wider than your shoulders, knees bent, hips down. A tall post player gets pushed out of the lane.
  • Seal your defender. Put your backside into their legs and keep them on your back. Your arm closest to the ball is a target; your other arm holds your space.
  • Show a big target. Hands up, fingers spread, calling for the ball. Your guard should know exactly where to throw it.

The move in three pieces

  1. Catch and feel. Catch with two hands and chin the ball, elbows out. Don't look back yet. Feel where your defender is pressing. If they're leaning on your top side (away from the baseline), the baseline is open.
  2. Drop and seal. Swing your baseline foot back and around the defender's leg. Your hip should end up in front of their body. One strong step, not a slide.
  3. Step and finish. Bring your other foot through toward the rim and go up strong with both hands, ball away from the defender. Use the backboard.

Why it works: Your defender has already committed their weight to one side. The drop step goes to the side they've given up. You don't need to be faster or taller; you just need to read what they've given you.

Common mistakes I see

  • Dribbling first. A dribble in the post gives the defense time to recover and lets guards dig at the ball. If you're sealed, you don't need it. If you do dribble, make it one hard dribble between your feet as you drop.
  • Turning to look. Looking over your shoulder tells the defender what you're about to do. Learn to feel the contact instead.
  • Traveling. Your other foot is your pivot foot. Keep it planted until you drop. Slow reps fix this fast.
  • Fading away. After the drop, your shoulders should face the baseline or the rim, not the sideline. Go up, not out.

How to practice it at home

You don't need a defender to start. Put a chair just outside the block on each side of the lane.

  • Toss the ball to yourself off the backboard or have a parent pass it to you.
  • Catch with your back to the basket, chin the ball, count "one."
  • Drop step around the chair and finish. Five makes on each side.
  • Then add a partner holding a pad or a pillow to lean on you, and react to which side they push.

Ten minutes a day for two weeks will change how comfortable your player feels with their back to the basket. And when they get bigger, faster defenders later on, the footwork will already be there.
