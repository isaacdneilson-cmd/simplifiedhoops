title: How to finish with your weak hand: why young players need both hands at the rim date: 2026-11-17 minutes: 5 art: dropstep summary: If your player only finishes with one hand, defenders will figure it out fast. Here's how to build a weak-hand layup step by step, and why it matters. ---

Watch a youth game and count how many layups come from the left side of the rim with the right hand. Players twist back to their strong hand, and the shot gets blocked. By high school, defenders know it's coming. They sit on that strong hand and force the player to finish with the other one. Learning to use both hands around the rim is one of the fastest ways a young player can score more.

Why the weak hand matters so much

Finishing with the hand farthest from the defender puts your body between the defender and the ball. That's the whole idea. On the right side of the rim, that's your right hand. On the left side, it's your left. If you can only use one hand, half the time you're finishing with the ball right next to the shot blocker. You're also slower, because you have to adjust to get back to your good side. A player who can finish with either hand doesn't have to think about it. They just go up.

I played my college ball around the rim as a post player at BYU and Utah Valley, and as a defender, the idea is simple: take away the strong hand. A finisher who can use either hand takes that option away from the defense.

The foundation: footwork before the hand

Most weak-hand layups fail before the ball ever leaves the hand. The feet are wrong. So we start there, without a ball.

  • Opposite foot, opposite hand. For a left-hand layup, you jump off your right foot. For a right-hand layup, you jump off your left. This is the piece that feels strange at first, and it's the one that matters most.
  • Knee drives up with the shooting hand. On a left-hand layup, the left knee comes up as you jump. That knee protects the ball and helps you go up, not out.
  • Jump up, not forward. If you're flying toward the baseline, you lose control of the shot. Your takeoff should carry you to the rim, not past it.

We walk through the steps slowly, saying "right, left, up" or "left, right, up" until the rhythm feels normal.

Step one: the hand and the release

Now we add the ball, starting right at the rim with no steps at all.

  • Stand a step from the basket, on the side of your weak hand.
  • Hold the ball with your shooting hand under it and your other hand on the side for protection.
  • Lift the ball high, keeping it near your shoulder, not out in front of your chest where it can be swiped.
  • Release it soft off the backboard, aiming for the top corner of the square.

Why start here? Because the weak hand doesn't trust itself yet. Close-range reps build touch without the player also worrying about footwork and speed. We want lots of makes so confidence builds.

Coach's rule: Right side, right hand. Left side, left hand. Jump off the opposite foot. If you remember those three things, your layups will look better within a week.

Step two: add the footwork

Once the release is soft and the ball is going in, we put the feet and hands together.

  1. One step. Start with your inside foot forward, take one step with the other foot, and go up.
  2. Two steps. Back up and take the full two-step approach into the layup.
  3. One dribble. Start a few feet out, take one dribble with your weak hand, and finish.

The dribble matters. If you dribble with your strong hand and then try to switch to your weak hand at the last second, the timing gets messy. Dribbling with the weak hand into a weak-hand finish keeps everything on the same side.

Step three: speed and pressure

Most players never practice the weak hand at game speed, so we push it one level at a time.

  • Full-speed drives. Same layup, but attacking from the wing or the top at game speed.
  • A pad or a hand in the way. A coach or parent gives a little bump as you go up. Players need to feel contact and still finish.
  • Live reps. One-on-one from the wing, where the defender can take away either side. Now you have to read it and use whichever hand is open.

If the weak hand misses everything at full speed, go back a level for a few days. That's normal.

How to practice at home

All you need is a hoop and ten minutes, a few days a week.

  • Back-and-forth layups: Stand under the basket and alternate layups, right hand on the right side, left hand on the left, without letting the ball hit the floor. Go for 20 makes.
  • Weak-hand only: Ten layups from the weak side, starting from one step, then two, then one dribble. Count makes.
  • Walk the feet: No hoop? Walk through the footwork in the driveway, saying the steps out loud, ten times each side.
  • Parent's job: Just watch the takeoff foot. If it's the wrong one, call it out. That one habit fixes most of the problem.

Your weak hand won't feel natural at first. That's fine. Break it down, build it one piece at a time, and keep getting reps. A player who can finish with both hands is a lot harder to guard, at every level.
