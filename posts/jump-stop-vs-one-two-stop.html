title: Jump stop vs. one-two stop: footwork basics every young player needs date: 2026-11-08 minutes: 5 art: iq summary: Travels, charges and rushed shots often start with how a player stops. Here's how to build the jump stop and the one-two stop, and when to use each. ---

A lot of the mistakes I see in youth games aren't really ball-handling or shooting problems. They're stopping problems. A player drives hard, can't stop under control, and ends up traveling, charging into a defender, or throwing up a rushed shot. How you stop decides what you can do next. So before we talk about moves, we learn two ways to stop: the jump stop and the one-two stop.

Why stopping matters so much

Every time you stop with the ball, you have to pick a pivot foot. That pivot foot decides which way you can turn, pass or shoot without traveling. The two stops give you different pivot options, and that's the whole reason to learn both. One gives you choices. The other gives you speed and rhythm.

The foundation: balance before anything else

Neither stop works if you land off balance. Before we add a ball, I want every player to be able to land and freeze.

  • Knees bent, hips down. Landing stiff-legged is how you tip forward and travel. Bent knees absorb your speed.
  • Feet about shoulder width. Too narrow and you wobble. Too wide and you can't move again quickly.
  • Chest up, head over your feet. If your head is out in front of your toes, your momentum is going to carry you forward.

We start by jogging and stopping on my call, then holding for a two-count. If a player has to take an extra step to catch themselves, we slow down until they can freeze every time.

Step one: the jump stop

In a jump stop, you take off from one foot or two and land on both feet at the same time. Think of a short, low hop, not a big leap.

  • Land on both feet together, on the balls of your feet, knees bent.
  • Keep the hop low. A high jump stop makes you land hard and lose control.
  • Ball goes right to your chin or your hip, protected and ready.

Why use it? Because both feet land together, you get to choose either foot as your pivot. That's a big deal in traffic. You can pivot away from a defender, step through, or turn either direction to pass. It's also the safest stop for a player who is still learning, because it's the hardest one to travel out of.

Step two: the one-two stop

In a one-two stop, also called a stride stop, you land one foot and then the other: right-left or left-right. The first foot that hits the floor becomes your pivot foot.

  • Land the first foot with a bent knee and plant it. That's your pivot.
  • Bring the second foot down a little in front, staying low and balanced.
  • Know which foot landed first. That's the one that can't come off the floor until you pass, shoot or start your dribble.

Why use it? It keeps your rhythm. Coming off a dribble into a pull-up jumper, or catching on the move and squaring up, the one-two stop lets you keep your momentum instead of stopping dead. Many shooters use it to get their feet set quickly.

The simple rule: Jump stop when you need options, like in a crowd, at the end of a drive, or catching in the post. One-two stop when you need rhythm, like going into a shot or attacking a closeout. The best players can do both without thinking about it.

Step three: add the pivot

A stop is only half the job. Once you can land balanced, add a pivot after every stop.

  • After a jump stop, practice front pivots and reverse pivots on both feet.
  • After a one-two stop, pivot only on the first foot down, and say it out loud at first so it becomes automatic.

This is where travels get fixed. Most traveling calls happen because a player isn't sure which foot is their pivot. When that's a habit, the whistle stops coming.

Adding speed and pressure

Once the pieces look solid, we make it harder one level at a time.

  1. Walking stops. Walk, stop on a call, hold balance. Both stops, both directions.
  2. Off the dribble. Dribble at half speed into each stop, then pivot.
  3. Full speed. Attack hard and stop under control. This is where most players fall apart, so we rep it a lot.
  4. With a defender. A partner shows up in your path. Stop, protect the ball, pivot and pass or shoot.

Don't move up until the current level looks easy. Speed without balance just means faster mistakes.

How to practice at home

You don't need a hoop for most of this. Ten to fifteen minutes is plenty.

  • Freeze drill: Jog across the driveway or yard. A parent calls "jump" or "one-two" and you stop that way and hold for two seconds. Twenty stops.
  • Dribble and stop: Dribble five steps, stop, pivot twice, repeat. Alternate stops and hands.
  • Name the pivot: After every one-two stop, say which foot is your pivot before you move.
  • Finish it: If you have a hoop, end each rep with a shot off the stop.

At 6'11", I had to learn early that footwork beats size. Big or small, the players who stop under control see the floor better, travel less and play with more confidence. It starts with knowing how to stop.
